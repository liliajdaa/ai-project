01

What is AI?

Fact There is no single agreed definition. The OECD describes an AI system as one that takes in data, interprets it for a goal, and produces an output: a prediction, a recommendation, a decision or new content.

Most of today’s AI is machine learning. Instead of following rules written by a programmer, the system learns patterns from enormous numbers of examples. A chatbot has processed billions of sentences, so it is very good at predicting which words come next.

My view Remember the demo above: AI predicts what is likely, not what is true. That is why it can sound confident and still be wrong.

02

Where it helps, where it hurts

Helps

  • Science. Fact AlphaFold predicts the 3D shape of proteins, work recognised by the 2024 Nobel Prize in Chemistry. It speeds up research into medicines.
  • Everyday work. Drafting, translating, summarising and coding in minutes instead of hours.
  • Me. As a branding and PR student, I used AI to build a working prototype of a fashion search website, something I could not have coded alone.

Hurts

  • Errors. Confident but wrong answers, often called hallucinations.
  • Bias. Systems learn from human data and can repeat its unfairness, for example in hiring or lending.
  • Privacy. Personal data trains these systems and is typed into them every day.
  • Deepfakes and fraud. Realistic fake video and voices (case A).
  • Dependence. When a few systems or providers fail, everything built on them fails too.
03

Two case studies

HK$200Mlost after one video call

The meeting where everyone was fake

Fact In January 2024, a finance employee at the Hong Kong office of engineering firm Arup joined a video call with the company’s chief financial officer and colleagues. Every other person on the call was a deepfake, built from publicly available video. Following their instructions, the employee made 15 transfers totalling HK$200 million (about US$25.6 million). The fraud surfaced only when the employee checked with head office (SCMP).

Why it worked: the employee first suspected the email was phishing, but seeing and hearing familiar faces removed the doubt.

My view The lesson is not “learn to spot fakes”, which keeps getting harder. It is to verify through a second channel: call back on a known number and require two approvals for large payments.

×2.3data centre electricity, 2024 → 2030

The electricity behind the chatbot

Fact Data centres used about 415 TWh of electricity in 2024, roughly 1.5% of the world’s total. The International Energy Agency projects about 945 TWh by 2030, about as much as Japan uses today, with AI as the main driver (Scientific American).

Fact The strain shows in PJM, the largest US grid. Its last capacity auction fell 6.8 GW short of the reserve target meant to limit unexpected outages to one every ten years. PJM expects data centres and other large users could add up to 70 GW by 2038 (Utility Dive). Regulators only partly approved its emergency plan to buy extra power in September 2026, mainly over who should pay (Utility Dive).

My view AI is not just software in the cloud. It runs on power plants and cables, and households may end up sharing the bill.

04

What could come next

↗

Opportunities

Prediction AI “agents” that complete whole tasks, faster scientific discovery and tutoring adapted to each student.

⚠

Security threats

Prediction Cheaper, more convincing fraud and cyberattacks, and agents making mistakes no one notices in time.

⏻

Outages and power

Prediction When a few cloud and AI providers fail, thousands of apps fail with them. Electricity may limit how fast AI grows.

◉

Human supervision

Fact The US NIST AI Risk Management Framework asks organisations to govern, map, measure and manage AI risks: know what your AI does, test it, watch it and plan for failure.

My view The people who benefit most will not be those who use AI the most, but those who check it best.

How this website was made

I kept two case studies, a fraud and the power grid, because they show AI’s human and physical sides. Labelling every claim as fact, view or prediction kept evidence and opinion apart. The look borrows from risograph posters: condensed type, two inks and offset shadows. The next-word demo makes the key idea, likely is not true, something you can try rather than just read.

The page uses semantic HTML (header, nav, main, section, article, figure). CSS custom properties hold the colours; CSS Grid and clamp() adapt the layout from phone to desktop. JavaScript runs the demo and the case-study tabs, built with ARIA roles and arrow-key support. Without JavaScript, everything is still readable.
